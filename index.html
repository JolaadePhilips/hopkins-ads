Swift & Co. · c. 1895–1897 · Cotosuet shortening

The Cotosuet Carnival / The largest cake in the world

Retail spectacle + half-page newspaper ads · Chicago dailies; Rothschild & Co. store

[Newspaper announcement copy not recovered.] Hopkins' own account: “I will build there the largest cake in the world. I will advertise the cake in a big way in the newspapers… During the next week, 105,000 people climbed four flights of stairs to that cake… we had prizes to offer to those who guessed nearest to the weight, but every guesser had to buy a pail of Cotosuet.”

Baker H. H. Kohlsaat & Co. built the cake in exchange for buying a carload of Cotosuet. Hopkins on his own method: “I did not sell Cotosuet, did not talk Cotosuet. Dr. Shoop · c. 1899–1904 · Guarantee system

Your own druggist signs the warrant

Booklets, dealer pieces, later newspapers · ~400,000 booklets mailed per day at peak

Hopkins' own paraphrase — he writes “My ads said in effect”: “Try this cough remedy; watch the benefits it brings. It cannot harm, for no opiates are in it. If it succeeds, the cough will stop. If it fails, it is free. Your own druggist signs the warrant.”

THIS IS A PARAPHRASE, NOT A HEADLINE — Hopkins explicitly writes “in effect.” Do not quote it as period copy. The insight is the real artefact: a distant advertiser's guarantee is worthless; a neighbourhood druggist's is not. Mailing lists covered heads of families in some 86,000 post offices.

Memoir only Source: Hopkins, My Life in Advertising (1927), pp. 75–77 ↗

Schlitz · 16 Mar 1901 · Schlitz Beer

The Extreme of Purity

Magazine ad, full page · Harper's Weekly

You who visit the Schlitz brewery marvel first at its cleanliness. No wonder the beer is pure! Then we show you a plate-glass room where we cool it. The very air of that room has been filtered. You smile at the thought that common air is not pure enough. Then we show the filter through which all Schlitz beer must go. You wonder what there can be to filter out. Then we show you how we bottle and seal it; then actually sterilize every bottle. Brewing — like digestion — turns barley starch into sugar; and a saccharine product is a breeding place for germs. Schlitz · 20 Apr 1901 · Schlitz Beer

How Schlitz Beer is Brewed

Magazine ad · Harper's Weekly

There's a vast difference in beers — a difference as great as between healthfulness and harm. You who drink it should know it. We have no wish to condemn any beer or its maker; but simply let us tell you how Schlitz beer is brewed. The yeast that we use never varies. It is always developed from the same mother cells. The yeast is so perfect that the cells are priceless. We insist on absolute purity. Schlitz beer is even cooled in filtered air. Then we filter the beer; then we sterilize it, after it has been bottled and sealed. Every process of our brewing is supervised by a partner in our concern.

Earliest verbatim “mother cells” ad located. Also signed J. L. STACK. The whole preemptive-claim doctrine sits in one line: “We have no wish to condemn any beer or its maker; but simply let us tell you how Schlitz beer is brewed.” Every brewer did these things. Only Schlitz said so.

Dr. Shoop · c. 1902–1903 · Dr. Shoop's Restorative

Here are the names of 1,000 Chicago people whom I have cured

Sunday newspaper page ad · Chicago Sunday papers

Probably the names of some of your friends are among them. [Each patient's address was printed alongside. The ad also stated that in curing those 1,000 the doctor had failed with seventeen.]

Schlitz · 17 Jul 1902 · Schlitz Beer

Poor Beer vs. Pure Beer

Magazine ad, two-column comparison · Life

Both cost you alike, yet one costs the maker twice as much as the other. One is good, and good for you; the other is harmful. Let us tell you where the difference lies. — POOR BEER is easy to brew. The materials are cheap. Cleanliness is not important, for the users never see it brewed. Any water will do. No air is too impure for the cooling. No filtering, no sterilizing; almost no ageing, for ageing ties up money. What is the use of expense and care when there is no reputation to defend? — PURE BEER calls for the best materials. The brewery must be as clean as your kitchen. The cooling must be done in filtered air, in a plate glass room. The product must be aged for months. The beer must be filtered, then sterilized in the bottle. — Schlitz is a pure beer, famous for fifty years. To maintain its standard, we double the necessary cost of our brewing.

Never names a rival, yet indicts the entire category. The line “the users never see it brewed” is the hinge of the whole campaign.

Liquozone · early 1903 · Liquozone

[The founding split test — creative not preserved]

Newspaper ads in a dozen small Illinois cities · A dozen small Illinois cities

“We will pay the price.” The offer: a 50c bottle free, redeemed by an order on a named local druggist; then a guaranty of six dollar bottles for five dollars, signed by that druggist, money back if unsatisfactory.

THE FOUNDING TEST OF MODERN TEST MARKETING. Documented economics: enquiries cost 18 cents each; the 30-day sale was 90 cents per enquirer; guaranty claims ran about 1 per cent of sales. Hopkins joined a company with a negative $45,000 net worth for no salary and a quarter interest.

Liquozone · 18 Nov 1905 · Liquozone

[The exposé — what the product actually was]

Magazine exposé · Collier's Weekly, later collected in The Great American Fraud

Samuel Hopkins Adams' analysis, published in Collier's: sulphuric acid about nine-tenths of one per cent; sulphurous acid about three-tenths of one per cent; water nearly ninety-nine per cent. Lederle Laboratories, October 1905, found Liquozone had no curative effect and that animals given it in pure form died slightly earlier than untreated ones.

INCLUDED DELIBERATELY. Adams names Hopkins directly — “perhaps the ablest exponent of his specialty in the country” — while also noting he was not responsible for the basic fraud, which predated him. This is the best period attribution document for Hopkins' early career AND the moral bill for it. Liquozone was the peak of his technique applied to a worthless product; a swipe file that omits it is lying by selection.

Van Camp's · c. 1908–1912 · Pork and Beans

[The strategy: argue against home baking]

— · Hopkins, My Life in Advertising (1927), p. 98

From the memoir: “I found that 94 per cent of the housewives baked their own pork and beans. Only 6 per cent were amenable to any canned-bean argument. Yet all the advertisers of pork and beans were merely crying, ‘Buy my brand.' I started a campaign to argue against home baking. I told of the sixteen hours required to bake beans at home. I told why home baking could never make beans digestible. I pictured home-baked beans, with the crisped beans on top, the mushy beans below. I told how we selected our beans, of the soft water we used, of our steam ovens where we baked beans for hours at 245 degrees. Then I offered a free sample for comparison. The result was an enormous success.”

THE BEST STRATEGIC LESSON IN THE FILE. Every rival fought over the 6% who already bought canned beans. Hopkins went after the 94% who baked their own. Note: the figure is 245 degrees — the widely-circulated “145 degrees” is an OCR error in the archive.org text of the 1927 edition, corrected against the page image.

Van Camp's · c. 1912–1918 · Evaporated Milk

Corrections — things everyone repeats that are wrong

  • Schlitz bottles were “washed with live steam.”
    Never appeared in a Schlitz ad. A sweep of 6,739 issues of Life, Harper's Weekly, Judge, Collier's, Cosmopolitan, Literary Digest, Saturday Evening Post, Ladies' Home Journal and Printers' Ink found zero instances of “live steam” anywhere near “Schlitz.” The real claim, in Life of 12 Dec 1907 and in both of Hopkins' own books, is that every bottle was washed four times by machinery. The nearest steam reference is barrels “cleaned with steam and soda” in 1900. The phrase circulates today from an uncited web page and has been repeated ever since.
  • Schlitz drew water from wells 4,000 feet deep.
    1,400 feet, and there were six of them. Six separate insertions across Life and Harper's Weekly between 1903 and 1907 all print 1400. Hopkins' memoir says 4,000. The ads win.
  • The Puffed Grains ads claimed 125 million steam explosions.
    The ads said 100 million explosions and 125 million food cells. Two different figures attached to two different nouns. Hopkins compressed them into one line years later in both books. The 1909 ads carry no count at all — the number was a later refinement, first appearing in 1914. The widely-quoted “12 million steam explosions” has no period support whatsoever.
  • “Food Shot From Guns” launched in 1913.
    January 1909. Newspaper full text shows the line running as display advertising from 26 January 1909. Nothing found in 1908. The 1913 date appears to be an error propagated between modern retellings.
  • Goodyear's All-Weather Tread campaign began around 1911.
    October 1913. A sweep of ~2,100 issues covering 1908–1918 found zero All-Weather ads before 25 Oct 1913 — which explicitly headlines it “Another Goodyear Invention.” Through 1911–13 the standing tagline is “With or Without Non-Skid Treads.”
  • Hopkins' first Overland ads were headed “The Wonderful Overland Story.”
    That was the booklet's title, not a headline. In the surviving 1910 artefacts it is the name of the coupon-requested book. The actual headlines are “Ask for the Story,” “The Record of Records” and “The Car That Captured the Country.” Hopkins' memoir claim should not be repeated uncorrected.
  • Hopkins wrote the Ovaltine advertising.
    False. Wrong agency, wrong decade. “Ovaltine,” “Wander,” “malted” and “cocoa” appear zero times in the full text of My Life in Advertising. Across 641 issues of Printers' Ink 1904–1924 the Wander Company appears exactly once: as a founding client of Blackett & Sample in May 1923. The confusion likely comes from Hill Blackett being an ex-Lord & Thomas man. This entry was in the earlier spreadsheet and should be deleted.
  • Hopkins wrote the Listerine halitosis campaign.
    False. “Listerine” and “Lambert” appear nowhere in his memoir, and no trade-press item connects him to the account. The campaign is documented to Milton Feasley and Gordon Seagrove with company president Gerard B. Lambert, from about 1923.
  • Hopkins worked on Gillette, Horlick's, Sunkist, Bon Ami, Kotex, Cutex or Chapin & Gore.
    No evidence for any of them. Gillette ran through a succession of other agencies (Donnelly, Tobey-Jones, Street & Finney, Arnold & Dyer, Presbrey, Geo. L. Dyer). Horlick's was J. Walter Thompson's, continuously, including during Hopkins' Racine years. Bon Ami was A. W. Erickson's, and the “Hasn't scratched yet” chick is documented to the client's own manager. Sunkist was a Lord & Thomas account but no period source names any copywriter, and citrus appears nowhere in Hopkins' memoir. Kotex reached Lord & Thomas only in October 1923, months before he left. Chapin & Gore appears zero times in ~1,000 issues of Printers' Ink.
  • Hopkins retired in 1923 or 1924.
    He worked until the year he died. He resigned Lord & Thomas in May 1924, then: advisory role at Kling-Gibson from July 1924; chairman of the board of The Quinlan Company from June 1928; chairman of the executive committee of the merged Kling-Gibson-Quinlan from April 1929; carried into Critchfield & Company in September 1931. He also ran a self-advertised consultancy from Fruitport, Michigan, placing his own ads in Printers' Ink as late as August 1932. His last documented campaign ran in June 1932. He died that September.
  • The surviving Hopkins corpus contains 463 ads. Or 1,621. Or 467.
    All vendor marketing. No archival census exists. Every circulating number traces to sales copy for paid swipe-file products, with no published methodology, no item-level index and no provenance. The structural problem is that period advertisements carried no copywriter or agency credit at all — so attributing any given ad to Hopkins personally is inference, not documentation. He also supervised staff at Lord & Thomas, which makes “a Hopkins ad” ambiguous even in principle. A provenance-checked set in the dozens is worth more than an unsourced claim of thousands.
  • Mrs. Hopkins inspired the Pepsodent account.
    Not found in any source. His detailed Pepsodent chapters credit a dentist's film theory and his own coupon testing, and never mention his wife. The only “Mrs. Hopkins” in the 1923–33 trade press is Mrs. Hopkins' Pies of the New York Pie Baking Company. What his memoir does say is that an ambitious wife pushed him toward taking equity rather than commission — a different story entirely.
  • “Keep That Schoolgirl Complexion” was written by Hopkins in 1917.
    Dated to August 1919, author undocumented. The careful citation history places the first print appearance on 8 August 1919 and does not credit Hopkins. He was on the Palmolive account; whether he wrote this specific line is not established. Treat with caution — it is the most famous line attached to his name and the least evidenced.

Sources & archives

  • My Life in Advertising (1927), full text ↗
    The genuine Harper & Brothers first edition, digitised from the University of California copy, marked NOT_IN_COPYRIGHT and fully open. The single richest source on this page — chapters on Bissell, Swift, Dr. Shoop, Schlitz, Liquozone, Van Camp's, Goodyear, Palmolive, Puffed Grains and Pepsodent.
  • Scientific Advertising (1923), facsimile PDF ↗
    The 102-page book Lord & Thomas registered as a pamphlet in May 1923, with the agency — not Hopkins — as copyright claimant. Free full text.
  • Printers' Ink, complete run 1888–1935 ↗
    THE MOST UNDERUSED SOURCE FOR THIS SUBJECT. The leading US advertising trade weekly, which reproduced, credited and criticised Hopkins' campaigns as they ran. 2,400+ issues, unrestricted, with downloadable OCR.
  • Saturday Evening Post ↗
    6,000+ digitised issues. The highest-circulation general weekly of the era, and where the Chalmers, Hudson, Reo and Goodyear campaigns actually ran.
  • Collier's, Ladies' Home Journal, Life, Harper's Weekly ↗
    The other four magazines that carry most of the surviving Hopkins-era ads. LHJ is where the Bissell and Palmolive work ran; Life and Harper's Weekly carry the entire Schlitz purity campaign; Collier's carries the auto and Puffed Grains work.
  • Pennsylvania Newspaper Archive ↗
    CRITICAL WORKAROUND. Runs the same software and JSON API as Chronicling America but is not behind a bot challenge. Source of the transcribed 1920 Pepsodent, 1909 Puffed Grains and 1910 Overland ads on this page.
  • Other open Chronicling America mirrors ↗
    DigitalNC (North Carolina), Nebraska Newspapers, NYS Historic Newspapers and South Carolina run the same API on the same corpus. Between them they yielded the dated Schlitz, Palmolive and Quaker newspaper plates.
  • Chronicling America / Library of Congress ↗ (bot-blocked to scripts; works in a browser)
    The canonical US newspaper archive, and the obvious place to look.
  • Judicious Advertising & Agricultural Advertising (Lord & Thomas house organs) ↗
    Lord & Thomas published its own trade magazines. HathiTrust holds ~49 full-view volumes of Judicious Advertising covering 1906–1925 and 26 volumes of Agricultural Advertising covering 1901–1918 — all pre-1929 and freely readable.
  • Swiped.co — Claude Hopkins ↗
    Six curated Hopkins items, each with a scan plus editorial breakdown: Food Shot From Guns (1909), the calling-out-audience ads (1909), Palmolive 5 Mistakes (1925–26), the Pepsodent 10 Day Test (1923), the Lord & Thomas $1000/week house ad (1909), and Schlitz.
  • The Vault — three large Hopkins PDF collections ↗
    The largest free aggregation of purported Hopkins ads on the open web: three public-domain PDFs totalling roughly 654 MB.
  • Rob Schorman on Hopkins and Bissell (free full text) ↗
    The best peer-reviewed scholarship on Hopkins' earliest work, reconstructing his 1889–1894 Bissell campaigns from original archival scrapbooks. Journal of the Gilded Age and Progressive Era 7:2 (2008), 181–219.
  • Samuel Hopkins Adams, “Liquozone” (1905) ↗
    The Collier's exposé that names Hopkins directly as “perhaps the ablest exponent of his specialty in the country” — while noting he did not devise the underlying fraud. Includes the chemical analysis showing the product was about 99% water.
  • Public Museum of Grand Rapids — Bissell Archival Collection ↗
    Holds the Bissell company scrapbooks containing Hopkins' earliest surviving advertisements. The documented physical home of the earliest verifiable Hopkins corpus.
  • Duke — Hartman Center for Sales, Advertising & Marketing History ↗
    Holds Lord & Thomas house advertisements and subject files on former staff including John E. Kennedy and Claude Hopkins.
  • The Lasker Story: As He Told It (1963) ↗
    Openly downloadable, unlike every other Lasker biography. Reproduces verbatim stenographer's notes of a Lord & Thomas staff meeting of 4–5 April 1925, in which Lasker discusses Hopkins' departure a year after it happened.
